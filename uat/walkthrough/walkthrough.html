Before you start

AssetCore is an asset-management and maintenance system for gas infrastructure: it keeps the register of every physical asset, schedules the maintenance and inspections that keep them safe, turns what inspectors find into jobs, and tracks the money, compliance and risk around all of it. These two cards get you signed in and set up so the rest of the session runs without a lockout.

What it is: the Operations Dashboard is the first thing everyone lands on — a live summary of the whole network. Network Health is the average condition score across assets; the tiles count assets, open work, overdue preventive maintenance and compliance alerts; Active Alerts lists what needs attention now. The left-hand menu is filtered by role, so what you see as System Admin is the fullest view anyone gets.

Set up the organisation

Before any asset can exist, the organisation needs its geography and its vocabulary: locations (a region or state), sites inside them (a physical facility), categories (what kind of asset something is), the people who will use it, and the rule for who signs off spending. All of this lives under Admin.

What it is: the location hierarchy. A location is a region — Lagos, Delta — and a site is a real facility inside it, like a metering yard or compressor station. Every asset sits on a site, and a user can be limited to certain locations or sites, which is how a regional engineer only sees their own patch.

The asset register

The asset register is the heart of the product: one record per physical asset, with its identity (the AIN — asset identification number), where it is, what it is worth, when it was last and next maintained, and a live health score built from inspections, defects, overdue work and risk. Assets can be entered by hand, imported from a spreadsheet, labelled with QR codes and found in the field by scanning.

What it is: the full asset record. The starred fields are required; the rest feed other parts of the product — value and useful life drive depreciation, the maintenance dates drive the calendar, and the coordinates put the asset on the map.

Stores and planned maintenance

Spare Parts is the parts store: what is on the shelf, what it cost, and every movement in or out, so stock can be reserved against a job and drawn when the job closes. Maintenance holds the preventive schedules — the recurring work that stops things breaking — which generate tasks on their own at the frequency you set.

What it is: each part carries a reorder level. When stock falls to it or below, the part is flagged Reorder. Every change is recorded as a movement — a receipt, an issue, a return or an adjustment — so the balance always has a reason behind it.

The core loop: inspection → defect → job

This is the chain the product exists for. An inspection checks an asset and rates its condition 1–5; what it finds goes on the defect register; a defect becomes a work order (a job); the job is assigned, worked, costed, signed off and closed; closing it resolves the defect and moves the asset's health. Four roles take part — run it as the hand-off it really is, with your colleague as the technician.

What it is: inspections are the eyes on the network — routine condition checks, safety inspections, regulatory integrity checks. Attaching one to an asset is what lets its rating reach that asset's health score. (Checklist templates exist in the system but there is no screen to create one yet, so this inspection records free-text findings.)

Money: depreciation

Depreciation writes an asset's value down over its useful life, so the register shows what equipment is actually worth today — its net book value (NBV). AssetCore keeps it as a proper schedule of yearly charges that are posted, like entries in a ledger, so the book value always has an audit trail.

What it is: the preview shows the full year-by-year schedule before anything is saved, worked out from the asset's own record. Straight-line spreads the value evenly: (cost − salvage value) ÷ useful life, per year.

Compliance and risk

Compliance tracks the licences and certificates an operator must hold — each with an issuing authority and an expiry date, flagged well before it lapses — and the audits carried out against standards like ISO 9001. The risk register scores what could go wrong on a 5×5 grid of likelihood against consequence, twice: before controls (inherent) and after them (residual).

What it is: the licence register is how an operator avoids running a facility on a lapsed permit. Anything inside its expiry window is flagged Expiring, and counted on the dashboard's Compliance Alerts tile.

Oversight: seeing the whole picture

The rest of the product is how managers watch the network without opening records one at a time: reliability figures and a map, a calendar of everything scheduled, exportable reports, automatic chasing of anything left too long, connections to field devices and other systems, and a tamper-proof log of who did what.

What it is: the maintenance KPIs. MTBF (mean time between failures) is how long assets run between breakdowns; MTTR (mean time to repair) is how long a repair takes once started; Availability is uptime as a percentage; the backlog sorts open work by age. Every figure shows its sample size, so a number built on one job never passes for a trend.

Who can do what

Permissions are enforced by the server, not just by hiding buttons. For each role, check what it should see, then type a forbidden address straight into the browser — the page must refuse, not load. This is the most common way real systems leak data, which is why it gets its own section.

What it is: the read-only role for leadership. It can open every operational page but create, edit or approve nothing, and it has no access to Admin or the audit log.

Things only a person can settle

Three rounds of automated testing could not prove these, because the testing tool's simulated keyboard does not behave like a real one. With a physical keyboard and a phone they take five minutes, and they close the last open questions from earlier rounds.
